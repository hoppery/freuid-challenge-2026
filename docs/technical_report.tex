\begin{abstract}
The FREUID Challenge 2026 requires a scalar fraud score in $[0,1]$ for each identity-document image, ranked
by the FREUID Score (AuDET $+$ APCER@1\%BPCER; lower is better). The private split, which decides the
ranking, is born-digital and contains identity-document types that are not present in the training data. The
central difficulty is therefore cross-type generalization: a detector must judge a document type it has never
seen without flagging a genuine one simply because it looks unfamiliar. Our submitted model is the
probability average of two full fine-tuned RGB detectors, a ViT-L/14 DINOv2 transformer and a ConvNeXt-V2-L
convolutional network, both trained at 896 pixels on the FREUID training split only, with no external data.
Two design choices target unseen types. First, Fourier Domain Adaptation randomizes the low-frequency global
appearance during training, which decouples the fraud signal from document-type look. Second, we combine a
transformer and a convolutional backbone, which rely on different cues and fail on different document types,
so their average holds up where either alone would slip. A tail-margin loss focuses training on the
low-BPCER tail that the metric scores. In leave-one-document-type-out cross-validation on the two hardest
folds, the two backbones are complementary (prediction correlation about 0.78 to 0.85), and their average
lowers the two-fold mean FREUID Score from about 0.0084 for a single backbone to 0.0032, reaching 0.0002 on
the EGYPT fold. Inference is a plain probability average in a pinned container that runs offline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Identity-document fraud detection asks whether a document image is genuine or fraudulent. The FREUID
Challenge~\cite{freuid2026} frames this as a single scalar task: the model outputs
$P(\text{fraudulent})\in[0,1]$ for each image, scored by the FREUID Score (AuDET $+$ APCER@1\%BPCER; lower is
better). The APCER@1\%BPCER term measures the attack-acceptance rate at a strict $1\%$ genuine-rejection
operating point, so the metric rewards a model that keeps a very low false-positive tail on genuine
documents.

The property that shapes our design is the gap between training and the private test in document type. The
training data covers five document types (EGYPT, GUINEA, BENIN, MOZAMBIQUE, and MAURITIUS driving licence and
identity cards) and is born-digital. The private split, which decides the ranking, is also born-digital but
includes document types that are not in the training data. A detector that memorizes the exact appearance of
the five training types can look strong in validation and still fail on the private split, because a genuine
document of an unseen type is unfamiliar and is easy to mistake for a manipulation. Cross-type generalization
is therefore the goal, not fitting the seen types more tightly.

We address this with one model and two design choices. Fourier Domain Adaptation~\cite{fda} randomizes the
low-frequency global appearance of training images, which pushes the network to decide on structure and
local evidence rather than on the overall color and layout of a given type. Architecture diversity pairs a
self-supervised vision transformer (ViT-L/14 DINOv2~\cite{dinov2,vit}) with a convolutional backbone
(ConvNeXt-V2-L). The two families carry different inductive biases, make different mistakes across document
types, and are only weakly correlated, so a plain probability average is more stable on unseen types than
either model alone~\cite{deepensembles}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item We build a cross-type fraud detector from FREUID training data only, with no external data, using
        Fourier Domain Adaptation to decouple the fraud signal from document-type appearance
        (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We show that a transformer and a convolutional backbone are complementary across document types:
        each is strong on the fold where the other is weak, and their predictions are decorrelated
        (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item We show, by leave-one-document-type-out cross-validation on the two hardest folds, that a plain
        probability average of the two backbones generalizes better across types than either single model
        (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:folds}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.}
Identity-document forgery detection and presentation-attack detection are active benchmark areas. For
synthetic and generative content, adapter-based detectors on frozen vision-language features generalize
across generators~\cite{fatformer}. Robustness to appearance shift has been approached by randomizing
feature statistics, for example MixStyle~\cite{mixstyle}, and by randomizing low-frequency image content, as
in Fourier Domain Adaptation~\cite{fda}, which we adopt. We use self-supervised transformer features
(DINOv2~\cite{dinov2}, ViT~\cite{vit}) together with a modern convolutional backbone (ConvNeXt-V2-L), both
from \texttt{timm}~\cite{timm}. Because the metric scores a strict operating point, we train with a loss that
focuses on the hard low-BPCER tail, related in spirit to focal loss~\cite{focal}. Our ensembling builds on
deep ensembles~\cite{deepensembles}; we add evidence that cross-architecture diversity, rather than
same-family seed diversity, is what helps on unseen document types.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview and problem setting}
\label{sec:setting}
Let $x$ be an image and $y\in\{0,1\}$ its fraud label. Each detector is an RGB backbone with a single-logit
head. It produces a fraud logit $\ell(x)$ and a probability $\sigma(\ell(x))\in[0,1]$, where $\sigma$ is the
sigmoid. The submitted score is the arithmetic mean of the two detectors' probabilities. Training data is
born-digital and covers the five seen document types; the private evaluation adds document types that are not
in training. Because the private data is not available at submission time, we do not fit to it; instead we
select the recipe by leave-one-document-type-out cross-validation on the training types
(Section~\ref{sec:data}). Every detector uses the same feed-forward pipeline: decode, resize to $896$ pixels
with ImageNet normalization, backbone, single-logit head, and sigmoid (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

\subsection{The two detectors}
\label{sec:models}
The submitted model averages two detectors that share the same recipe and differ only in backbone family.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{ViT-L/14 DINOv2}: backbone \texttt{vit\_large\_patch14\_reg4\_dinov2.lvd142m}, a
        self-supervised vision transformer, at $896$ pixels
        (\texttt{checkpoints/exp\_fdab12\_all/epoch2.pt}).
  \item \textbf{ConvNeXt-V2-L}: backbone \texttt{convnextv2\_large.fcmae\_ft\_in22k\_in1k}, a convolutional
        network, at $896$ pixels (\texttt{checkpoints/exp\_cnxfda\_all/epoch2.pt}).
\end{itemize}
Both are \texttt{model\_type=rgb}: the backbone feeds a single linear logit that a sigmoid maps to
$P(\text{fraud})$. There is no forensic residual branch and no auxiliary head. Both backbones are fully fine
tuned (the training config sets \texttt{freeze\_backbone=false}). We choose two families on purpose. A
self-supervised transformer and a convolutional network use different receptive fields and different global
context, so they tend to fail on different document types. That difference is what makes their average robust
on types neither has seen (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Fourier Domain Adaptation}
\label{sec:fda}
The main lever for cross-type generalization is Fourier Domain Adaptation (FDA)~\cite{fda}, applied as a
training augmentation. For a training image, we replace the low-frequency band of its amplitude spectrum with
that of another training image (the donor), keeping the original phase, and invert back to an image. The
band width is controlled by $\beta=0.12$. Amplitude at low frequencies carries the global color and layout
of a document type, while phase and high-frequency amplitude carry structure and fine detail. Swapping the
low-frequency amplitude therefore randomizes the overall look of the document while preserving the structure
and local evidence that separate genuine from fraudulent. The network is pushed to decide on cues that
transfer across types, so a genuine document of an unseen type is less likely to be flagged only because its
global appearance is unfamiliar.

\subsection{Tail-margin operating-point loss}
\label{sec:loss}
The FREUID Score includes APCER@1\%BPCER, the attack-acceptance rate at a strict $1\%$ genuine-rejection
operating point. Plain binary cross-entropy optimizes average separation, not this tail. We therefore train
with a tail-margin loss that adds a margin penalty on the hardest genuine examples near the operating
threshold, so the model pushes the high-scoring genuine tail below the threshold that the metric reads. In
probability space (\texttt{tail\_space=prob}), the loss uses a tail quantile of $0.99$ to locate the
threshold region, a margin of $1.0$, a weight $\lambda=1.0$, an offset $\mu=0.2$, and a $300$-step warmup
before the tail term is enabled (Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}). The full objective is the fraud binary
cross-entropy plus this tail-margin term. This loss does not change the architecture and adds no inference
cost.

\subsection{Architecture-diverse ensembling}
\label{sec:diversity}
A single backbone is strong on some document types and weak on others (Section~\ref{sec:results}). We reduce
this variance across types by averaging the two decorrelated backbones. The averaging is a plain arithmetic
mean of the two probabilities, with no weighting and no learned combiner. We do not add same-family seed
copies, because same-family models are highly correlated and their average barely moves the score. The
diversity that helps here is across architecture family, where the two members disagree on different document
types and the average cancels each member's type-specific errors.

\subsection{Score calibration}
\label{sec:calib}
Each detector outputs a fraud logit that a sigmoid maps to a per-model probability. The submission score is
the arithmetic mean of the two probabilities. We apply no temperature and no learned calibration, and no
post-processing differs between training and test time. Images that fail to decode are assigned $0.5$, so
every input gives exactly one finite score in $[0,1]$.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
We use the official FREUID \texttt{train/} images and \texttt{train.csv} metadata (Kaggle competition
\texttt{the-freuid-challenge-2026-ijcai-ecai}), assembled into a single manifest
\texttt{manifests/freuid.parquet}. The submitted model trains on this FREUID training split only. It uses
\textbf{no external data}: no captured-document corpora, no extra fraud sources, and no test data at any
point. The pretrained backbone weights are used under their upstream licenses.

\paragraph{Validation by leave-one-document-type-out.}
The private split contains document types that are not in training, so a random held-out split of the
training types would over-report cross-type performance. To estimate cross-type generalization with the data
we have, we use leave-one-document-type-out (LODO) cross-validation: we hold out one training document type
entirely, train on the other four, and score on the held-out type. This simulates the unseen-type condition,
because the held-out type is absent from training. We ran LODO across the training types and report the two
hardest folds, EGYPT and BENIN, in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The other held-out folds (GUINEA, MOZAMBIQUE,
and MAURITIUS) were easier and are not the bottleneck; we do not report per-fold numbers for them here. For
run logging we also hold out a random $8\%$ of the manifest during each training run
(\texttt{val\_fraction=0.08}). LODO is an estimate on training types held out one at a time, not a sample of
the private distribution, so it indicates the direction of cross-type generalization rather than a private
score.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}
The container entrypoint indexes a flat directory of images. It builds each checkpoint with
\texttt{pretrained=False}, loads the embedded weights, runs a forward pass, and applies a sigmoid.
Preprocessing is a plain resize to $896$ pixels with ImageNet normalization; there is no tiling, no
multi-crop, and no test-time augmentation. For each image it averages the two per-model probabilities and
writes \texttt{id,label}, where \texttt{label} is the average fraud probability in $[0,1]$. The device is
CUDA if available, else CPU. Images that fail to decode are assigned $0.5$. The exact command is in
Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
We report leave-one-document-type-out (LODO) FREUID Scores (lower is better) on the two hardest folds. The
two folds are hard for opposite reasons. EGYPT is appearance out-of-distribution: it has a distinct script
and color palette, so a genuine EGYPT document is easy to flag as unfamiliar. BENIN carries subtle
chroma-dependent attacks, where the separation between genuine and fraud lives in the color channels. No
single uniform treatment fixes both at once, which is why we look for two backbones that fail differently.

\subsection{The two backbones are complementary}
Table~\ref{tab:folds} and Fig.~\ref{fig:folds} show the pattern. The ViT-L/14 DINOv2 detector is strong on
EGYPT ($0.0020$) but weak on BENIN ($0.0140$). The ConvNeXt-V2-L detector is the reverse: strong on BENIN
($0.0023$) and weak on EGYPT ($0.0150$). Each model owns the fold that the other struggles with. Their
predictions are decorrelated, with a correlation between about $0.78$ and $0.85$ across the folds, which is
low enough for averaging to help. This is the cross-type version of the diversity effect: because the two
families fail on different document types, neither alone is safe for a type it has not seen.

\subsection{The ensemble generalizes across types}
The probability average is low on both folds. On EGYPT it reaches $0.0002$, below both single models, and on
BENIN it stays low at $0.0062$. The two-fold mean falls from about $0.0084$, the mean of the two single
backbones, to $0.0032$ for the ensemble. The point is not that the average wins every fold, but that it does
not collapse on either. A single backbone would be a risky choice for the private split, where the document
type is unknown in advance, because it could land on the fold it is weak on. Averaging the two decorrelated
backbones removes that risk in our LODO estimate, which is the behavior we want on unseen types. We do not
claim any leaderboard rank or any private-test score; we have none, and LODO on training types is an estimate
of direction, not a private measurement.

\subsection{Design choices we kept and dropped}
\label{sec:ablation}
Two choices carried the cross-type result. Fourier Domain Adaptation was kept because randomizing
low-frequency appearance is what lets an unseen-type genuine document avoid being flagged for looking
unfamiliar. Cross-architecture averaging was kept because it is the diversity that actually moves the score:
the two families disagree on different document types. We dropped same-family seed ensembling, because
same-family members are highly correlated and their average barely changes the result, unlike the
cross-architecture pair reported above. We also kept the model to FREUID training data only, since external
data did not target the born-digital, unseen-type condition that the private split presents.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
The reproducibility container ships the submitted two-model average. The two checkpoints are fetched from a
GitHub Release at build time, verified by SHA-256, and baked into the image, and both models build with
\texttt{pretrained=False}. The container therefore runs with \texttt{--network none} (no runtime downloads)
and writes only to \texttt{/submissions}.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Environment (pinned):} \texttt{torch==2.12.0} (CUDA 13), \texttt{timm==1.0.27},
        \texttt{albumentations==2.0.8}, \texttt{opencv-python-headless==4.13.0.92}, \texttt{numpy==2.4.6},
        \texttt{pandas==3.0.3}, \texttt{scipy==1.17.1}, \texttt{scikit-learn==1.9.0},
        \texttt{pillow==12.2.0}, \texttt{pyyaml==6.0.3}, \texttt{pyarrow==24.0.0}; Python $\geq 3.11$.
  \item \textbf{Container:} base image \texttt{nvidia/cuda:13.0.1-cudnn-runtime-ubuntu24.04}. Weights are
        fetched from the GitHub Release, SHA-256 verified, and baked in, so the image runs offline with
        \texttt{--network none}. Input is a flat \texttt{/data} directory of images; output is
        \texttt{/submissions/submission.csv} with columns \texttt{id,label}.
  \item \textbf{Hardware and runtime:} training on $2\times$ NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 (Blackwell, 96\,GB);
        inference uses a single GPU. The base image targets CUDA 13, which needs a host NVIDIA driver
        $\geq 580.65.06$; on a host with an older driver the container falls back to CPU, so adjust the base
        tag and the \texttt{torch} index to match (the checkpoint weights are torch-version portable).
  \item \textbf{Randomness:} global seed $42$. CUDA, cuDNN, and mixed precision add minor non-determinism.
  \item \textbf{Repository:} \url{https://github.com/hoppery/freuid-challenge-2026} (MIT license). The frozen
        commit SHA is stated in our reproducibility reply.
\end{itemize}
